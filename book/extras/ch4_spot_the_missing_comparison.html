Describe, then compare

Every Task 1 question ends with the same instruction: make comparisons where relevant. It is one of the most common reasons good writers lose marks. They describe each line accurately, one after another, and never say how the lines relate to each other.

Listing: Easton used 90 litres in 2004 and 150 litres in 2024. Westvale used 60 litres in 2004 and 45 litres in 2024.
Comparing: While water use in Easton rose by 60 litres, Westvale’s figure fell, so by 2024 Easton used more than three times as much.

Your turn

Rewrite the student’s answer. Keep the key figures, but make at least three comparisons, using at least two of the five ways above.

The line graph compares the average amount of water used per person each day in Northport, Easton and Westvale over the twenty-year period from 2004 to 2024.

Overall, water use rose in Northport and Easton but fell in Westvale. Northport had the highest consumption throughout, while Westvale’s was by far the lowest.

In 2004, the average resident of Northport used 150 litres a day, compared with 90 litres in Easton. Northport’s figure rose steadily to 180 litres in 2019 and then levelled off, while Easton’s grew faster, increasing by 60 litres in total compared with Northport’s 30. As a result, the gap between them halved, from 60 litres to 30.

Westvale followed the opposite trend. Its figure fell gradually from 60 litres to 45, and by 2024 water use in Northport was four times as high as in Westvale.

Before you finish, ask

  1. Does my overview compare the lines?
  2. Does every body paragraph compare, not just describe?
  3. Could a reader see which line was highest, which changed most, and what happened to the gap?
  4. Have I listed every year? Cut the middle figures and compare instead.
