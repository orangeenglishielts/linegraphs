Mastering Line Graphs · Chapter 1 extension
Foundation · Band 5–6

Describe the data, not the line

Why “the line goes up” loses marks, and how to say what actually changed.

Describe the data, not the picture

The examiner has the graph in front of them. What they are marking is how well you report the information: what was measured, how it changed and by how much. “The line goes up” describes the picture, not the people, money or things that were measured, so it shows the examiner very little.

Weak: The blue line goes up and then the red line goes down.
Better: The number of people who cycled to work rose, while car use fell.

You can look at the lines to find the story. When you write, describe what each line measures.

Three steps for every sentence

1 · Find what is measuredRead the title, the vertical axis and the legend.The legend says the solid line is “online”, and the axis says “% of adults”.
2 · Build the subjectthe number of + countable noun · the amount of + uncountable noun · the percentage / proportion of + nounthe percentage of adults who read the news online · the number of visitors · the amount of water used
3 · Add a trend verb, the figures and the timeThe percentage of adults who read the news online rose from 18% in 2004 to 74% in 2024.What, what happened, how much, when.

Instead of the line, say…

Don’t writeWrite
The line goes up.The number of visitors rose.
The line goes down a lot.Car use fell sharply.
The line is flat.Sales remained stable.
The line goes up and down.Prices fluctuated.
The line is at the top in August.Visitor numbers peaked in August.
The lines cross.Online reading overtook print reading.
The orange line / the dotted oneonline reading / the figure for Japan
Print is above online.Print reading was higher than online reading.
The line graph below shows…The line graph illustrates…
Japan is below Norway.The figure for Japan was lower than Norway’s.
Above and below make the reader picture the lines. To compare two figures, prefer higher than, lower than, more than or less than. (With a figure, fell below 20% is fine.)
In your introduction, don’t copy “below” from the task. The task says The graph below shows… because the graph is under the question on the exam paper. In your answer, “the graph above” or “the graph below” makes no sense. Write The line graph illustrates… or The line graph compares…
After the first mention, you don’t need the full subject every time. Use a shorter form: the figure for Japan, this figure, visitor numbers, online reading. Use it only when there is just one thing it could mean.

Numbers from the axis

Axis labels often say (millions) or (thousands). Students copy that word into their answer, and it comes out wrong.

Number + million / thousand · no -s10 million, 2.5 million, 4 thousand, 3 hundredNever 10 millions or 4 thousands, even when the axis says “millions”.
…but the noun after it stays plural2 million books, 4,000 visitors, 3.5 million carsMillion, thousand and hundred lose the -s; the noun after them keeps it. Never 2 million book.
No number · millions ofMillions of people shopped online.Use “millions of” only with no number in front. After a number or a word like several, write million.
Easiest fix: for thousands and hundreds, write the full number. If the axis says (thousands) and the line is at 4, write 4,000.

Your turn

Rewrite this paragraph about the news graph. Don’t use the words line, solid, dashed or any colours.

The solid line starts at 18% and goes up to 74%. The dashed line goes down from 64% to 26%. The lines cross between 2012 and 2016. After 2020 the solid line only goes up a little.
The percentage of adults who read the news online rose from 18% in 2004 to 74% in 2024. Over the same period, the proportion who read the news in print fell from 64% to 26%. Online reading overtook print reading between 2012 and 2016. After 2020, growth in online reading slowed, with the figure rising by only four percentage points.

Before you write each sentence

  1. What does this line measure? Read the legend and the axis.
  2. Number, amount or percentage of what?
  3. What happened, how much, and when?
  4. Did I write “line”, a colour or “it”? Name the data, and compare with higher or lower than.
  5. Did I copy “millions” from the axis? Write 10 million books.
