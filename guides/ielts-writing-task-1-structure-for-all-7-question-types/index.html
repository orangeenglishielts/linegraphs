IELTS Writing Task 1 Structure for All 7 Question Types

By Lindsey, former IELTS examiner. 25 November 2026. Read this post with its interactive exercises.

IELTS Academic Writing Task 1 can show you a line graph, a map or a diagram of how chocolate is made. They look completely different, but you can use the same four-paragraph structure for all of them. What changes is the language inside each paragraph.

As a former IELTS examiner, I'd say a clear structure is the quickest win in Task 1. It helps the examiner find your overview, follow your comparisons and see that you've selected the main features.

The basics

  • Time: about 20 minutes (Task 2 is worth twice as much, so don't spend longer)
  • Length: at least 150 words
  • Your job: summarise the main features and make comparisons where relevant
  • Not your job: give opinions, explain causes or guess why something happened

The 4-paragraph structure

  1. Introduction (1 sentence): paraphrase what the visual shows.
  2. Overview (1 to 2 sentences): the main features, with no numbers.
  3. Body 1 (2 to 4 sentences): details and figures for one group of data.
  4. Body 2 (2 to 4 sentences): details and figures for the other group.

You don't need a conclusion. The overview does that job, and putting it second means the examiner sees it straight away. My post on the overview sentence explains why it matters so much.

The 7 question types

1. Line graph

Shows change over time. Your overview gives the overall trends and any crossover or peak.

  • Group by: lines that rose vs lines that fell, or the biggest vs smallest
  • Key language: rose steadily, fell sharply, peaked at, overtook

2. Bar chart

Can show change over time (dynamic) or a comparison at one moment (static). Check the axis first.

  • Group by: highest vs lowest, or categories that behave the same way
  • Key language: twice as high as, the second largest, whereas

Read: How to describe bar charts and static bar charts

3. Pie chart

Shows parts of a whole. Often two or more pies to compare.

  • Group by: the largest share vs the smallest, or what changed between pies
  • Key language: accounted for, made up, a quarter, the majority

Read: How to describe pie charts

4. Table

The same as a chart, but with numbers instead of shapes. Tables are harder because nothing stands out visually, so you have to find the patterns yourself.

  • Group by: rows or columns with the highest and lowest totals
  • Tip: circle the biggest and smallest number in each column before you write

5. Map

Usually two maps of the same place at different times. You describe what changed.

  • Group by: area (the north, the town centre) or by type of change (new buildings, things removed)
  • Key language: was replaced by, was converted into, was demolished, to the north of

Read: IELTS Task 1 maps: examples, grammar and practice

6. Process diagram

Shows the stages of how something is made or how something works. There are no numbers.

  • Overview: how many stages, and where it starts and ends
  • Group by: the first half of the process and the second half
  • Key language: the passive (is collected, is heated), sequencers (first, after that, finally)

7. Mixed charts

Two different visuals together, such as a line graph and a pie chart.

  • Overview: one main feature from each visual
  • Group by: one body paragraph per visual, or link them where the data connects

How the structure looks in practice

Here is a short example for a line graph about commuting times in three towns.

Introduction: The line graph shows how long it took people to commute to work in three towns between 2000 and 2020.

Overview: Overall, commuting times rose in all three towns, and Oakhaven changed from having the shortest journeys to the longest.

Body 1: In 2000, residents of Oakhaven spent around 20 minutes travelling to work, the lowest figure of the three. This more than doubled to 45 minutes by 2020.

Body 2: Journey times in Tarnbridge and Stellmoor rose more gradually, from 25 to 32 minutes and from 30 to 35 minutes respectively.

This is shortened to show the structure. A full answer adds more detail to each body paragraph and comes to at least 150 words.

The structure on test day

  1. Minutes 0 to 3: read the title, axes, units and legend
  2. Minutes 3 to 5: choose the main features and decide your two groups
  3. Minutes 5 to 8: write the introduction and overview
  4. Minutes 8 to 17: write the two body paragraphs
  5. Minutes 17 to 20: check numbers, units and tense

Practice activities

Activity 1: Match the type to the tip

Match each question type (1 to 7) with the most useful tip (a to g).

Question types: 1. Line graph · 2. Bar chart · 3. Pie chart · 4. Table · 5. Map · 6. Process diagram · 7. Mixed charts

  • a. Use the passive and words like "first" and "after that"
  • b. Describe what changed between the two dates
  • c. Check the axis first: is it static or dynamic?
  • d. Give one main feature from each visual in your overview
  • e. Circle the biggest and smallest number in each column
  • f. Use "accounted for" and "made up"
  • g. Look for crossovers and peaks

Answers (try first!): 1g · 2c · 3f · 4e · 5b · 6a · 7d

Activity 2: Which sentence doesn't belong?

These four sentences come from one Task 1 answer about book sales. One of them shouldn't be there. Which one, and why? Which sentence is the overview?

  • A. Overall, sales of e-books rose, while sales of printed books fell.
  • B. This is probably because people prefer reading on their phones.
  • C. In 2010, printed books accounted for 80% of sales.
  • D. The chart compares sales of printed books and e-books between 2010 and 2020.

Answers (try first!): B doesn't belong. It gives a reason, and Task 1 only asks you to report what the data shows. A is the overview: it gives the main trends with no numbers.
