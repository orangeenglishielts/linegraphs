10 Common Mistakes That Keep IELTS Writing Task 1 Scores at Band 6

By Lindsey, former IELTS examiner. 9 November 2026. Read this post with its interactive exercises.

Why am I stuck at Band 6 in IELTS Writing? Often, it is not because you need longer words or more complicated sentences. It is because recurring IELTS Task 1 mistakes make your report less complete, accurate or clear.

As a former IELTS examiner, I have seen how small habits can limit a response. Try this quick exercise. What are the main problems in this sample? Try to find at least two.

The chart shows information about transport in 2010 and 2020. Cars were 40 in 2010 and 55 in 2020. Buses were 30 and 25. I think this happened because more people could afford cars.

Answer: The sample repeats the question’s wording, lists figures without explaining the key comparison, and adds an unsupported opinion about why the figures changed. Each can weaken your response.

The official IELTS band descriptors explain what separates Band 6 from Band 7: among other things, a clear overview, well-selected key features, precise vocabulary and generally well-controlled grammar.

One format note before we start: whether you type your answers or handwrite them, your Writing is marked by an examiner against exactly the same criteria, so the ten habits below matter in either format.

Ten IELTS Task 1 mistakes to fix

1. Missing the overview, or burying it

What it looks like: You start describing individual figures, but never summarise the main trend or contrast. Or your overview appears after a long list of details.

Why it costs marks: Task Achievement. A clear overview is central to covering the main features of the visual.

Fix: State the most important overall pattern near the beginning.

Weak: “The figures for three transport types are shown below.”

Stronger: “Overall, car use increased, while bus use declined.”

2. Listing every number without grouping or comparison

What it looks like: “A was 20. B was 25. C was 30. D was 35.”

Why it costs marks: Task Achievement and Coherence and Cohesion. The figures may be accurate, but the reader has to work out which comparisons matter.

Fix: Group related data and explain the relationship.

Stronger: “D and C recorded the highest figures, at 35 and 30 respectively, while A and B were lower, at 20 and 25.”

Select key features; you do not need to report every data point.

3. Adding opinions, reasons or predictions

What it looks like: “This probably happened because the government improved the service,” or “The figure will continue to rise.”

Why it costs marks: Task Achievement. Task 1 asks you to report the information shown, not explain causes or predict what might happen.

Fix: Keep your statements anchored to the visual.

Weak: “More residents used buses because they are cheaper.”

Stronger: “Bus use increased over the period shown.”

4. Copying the question instead of paraphrasing it

What it looks like: Your opening sentence repeats the task prompt, changing only a word or two.

Why it costs marks: It makes your response less flexible and uses space without adding a useful overview.

Fix: Restate the visual’s subject and what it compares using accurate alternatives.

Question: “The chart shows the number of students choosing four subjects.”

Paraphrase: “The chart compares student enrolments across four academic subjects.”

Do not force synonyms that change the meaning. Accurate paraphrasing is better than unusual vocabulary.

5. Using the wrong tense

What it looks like: “The number of visitors increases from 2010 to 2020,” although both dates are in the past.

Why it costs marks: Grammatical Range and Accuracy. Tense errors can make the timing of the data unclear.

Fix: Check the time period before writing. Use the past simple for completed periods, the present simple for a chart describing a current situation, and appropriate future forms for projections.

Correct: “The number of visitors increased from 2010 to 2020.”

6. Confusing to and by with figures

What it looks like: “Sales rose by 80 in 2020,” when 80 is the final figure, not the amount of the increase.

Why it costs marks: Task Achievement and Lexical Resource. The reader may understand the wrong amount.

Fix: Use to for the final value and by for the size of the change.

Correct: “Sales rose to 80.”

Correct: “Sales rose by 20, from 60 to 80.”

7. Confusing percentages with percentage points

What it looks like: A share rises from 20% to 30%, and you write that it increased by 10%.

Why it costs marks: Task Achievement. The size of the change is reported inaccurately.

Fix: The difference between 20% and 30% is 10 percentage points. In relative terms, the 30% figure is 50% higher than 20%. Choose the description that matches the comparison you intend to make.

For more practice with fractions, proportions and comparisons, see the companion lesson Describing Percentages and Proportions in IELTS Writing Task 1.

8. Using inaccurate proportion language

What it looks like: “The majority of household spending went on housing,” when housing represents 40% of the total.

Why it costs marks: Lexical Resource and Task Achievement. The vocabulary overstates what the figures show. A majority means more than half.

Fix: Use “the largest proportion” or “the biggest share” for 40%.

Correct: “Housing accounted for the largest proportion, at 40%.”

For high-frequency topics such as transport, education, employment and household spending, practise precise collocations: the proportion of commuters, the share of the budget, the number of students and the rate of employment. Choose number for countable items and proportion/share for parts of a whole.

9. Writing fewer than 150 words

What it looks like: Your report ends at 130 words because you describe only a couple of figures.

Why it costs marks: You risk leaving key features undeveloped and failing to meet the stated task requirement.

Fix: Aim for at least 150 words, but prioritise relevance over padding. Add a clear overview, select key details and make useful comparisons. Do not repeat the same point to reach the count.

10. Relying on memorised sentences and overlooking grammar

What it looks like: A memorised phrase does not fit the visual, or it produces an inaccurate sentence such as “The chart gives information about the increase of people.”

Why it costs marks: Lexical Resource and Grammatical Range and Accuracy. Formulaic language can sound unnatural, while article and subject–verb errors reduce accuracy.

Fix: Adapt useful structures to the data and check each sentence’s subject and verb.

Weak: “The number of visitors were higher.”

Correct: “The number of visitors was higher.”

Correct: “Visitor numbers were higher.”

Also check articles: “The chart shows the proportion of students”; “Housing accounted for the largest share.”

IELTS Writing Task 1 Band 6 vs 7: a worked example

Imagine a bar chart showing the percentage of commuters travelling by car, bus and bicycle in 2010 and 2020.

Flawed sample answer

The bar chart gives information about how people travelled in 2010 and 2020. In 2010, cars were 50%, buses were 30% and bicycles were 20%. In 2020, cars were 60%, buses were 25% and bicycles were 15%. The car figure went up by 10%. Bus and bicycle figures went down. I think people used cars more because they are convenient. This trend will probably continue in the future.

What limits it: The introduction closely copies the task. There is no clear overview. The report lists each number before drawing a comparison, uses “went up by 10%” imprecisely, and adds an opinion and prediction. The phrase “cars were 50%” is also less natural than “50% of commuters travelled by car”.

Improved sample answer (shortened for space)

The bar chart compares the proportions of commuters who travelled by car, bus or bicycle in 2010 and 2020. Overall, the car became more popular, while the shares travelling by bus and bicycle declined. It remained the most common form of transport in both years. In 2010, half of commuters travelled by car, compared with 30% who took the bus and 20% who cycled. By 2020, the proportion travelling by car had risen to 60%, an increase of 10 percentage points. Bus use fell by five percentage points to 25%, while the figure for cycling dropped to 15%.

The revised report paraphrases the task, gives a direct overview, groups the main comparisons and reports the percentage-point changes accurately. It does not speculate about reasons or future trends. These changes reflect the differences between Band 6 and Band 7 descriptors, but no single template guarantees a particular score.

Practise spotting IELTS Task 1 common errors

Error-spotting set

Find at least six problems in this report:

The diagram shows the number of students choosing art, science and history in 2010 and 2020. In 2010, art was 40, science was 35, and history was 25. In 2020, art was 45, science was 30, and history was 25. The majority of students chose art because it is more interesting. I think science will become less popular. The chart give a lot of information about students.

Check your answers: The opening copies the task; there is no overview; the response lists figures without grouping; “the majority” is inaccurate if art represents 45 out of 100; the reason and prediction are unsupported; and “chart give” needs subject–verb agreement. Add units if the visual provides them.

Fix the overview

Rewrite this weak overview:

“Overall, there were different numbers in the three categories.”

A stronger version might be:

“Overall, art attracted the most students in both years, while history remained unchanged and science declined.”

Your overview should highlight the most important pattern, not repeat every figure.

Self-check quiz

  1. A figure rises from 25% to 35%. What is the percentage-point increase?
  2. If a value changes from 40 to 55, which preposition introduces the final figure: to or by?
  3. Is 40% usually a majority?
  4. Should a Task 1 report include a prediction if the chart gives no forecast?

Answers: 1. 10 percentage points. 2. To. 3. No. 4. No.

Make your study buddy a second pair of examiner eyes

Swap reports with a friend and check each other’s work using the ten mistakes above. First, mark the overview and underline the key comparisons. Then circle any unsupported claims, check tense and figures, and count the words. Ask your partner to explain one change they made and why.
