IELTS Bar Chart Vocabulary: Words and Phrases for Writing Task 1

By Lindsey, former IELTS examiner. 25 November 2026. Read this post with its interactive exercises.

As a former IELTS examiner, I'd say the vocabulary in a bar chart answer matters as much as reading the chart correctly. If you describe every bar with "was" and "had", the examiner sees very little range, even when your ideas are right.

Bar charts need slightly different language from line graphs. A line graph is usually about change over time. A bar chart is usually about comparison: which category is bigger, smaller, highest, lowest, or roughly the same. Sometimes it shows change too, so you need both sets of words.

This post gives you the vocabulary in groups, with an example sentence for each, all based on one example chart.

Example chart: the percentage of adults in four countries who read a book every week, in 2010 and 2020.

1. Words for what the chart shows

Start your introduction by paraphrasing the question. These verbs and nouns help you avoid copying it.

  • compares, shows, illustrates, gives information about
  • the proportion of / the percentage of / the number of

The bar chart compares the proportion of adults in four countries who read a book weekly in 2010 and 2020.

Use "compares" when there are two or more groups side by side. It tells the examiner you've understood the purpose of the chart.

2. Comparing two bars

This is the language you will use most.

  • higher than / lower than
  • more than / fewer than (countable) / less than (uncountable)
  • twice as high as, almost three times as many as
  • compared with / in comparison with
  • whereas / while (to contrast two figures in one sentence)

In 2010, the figure for Norway (62%) was more than twice as high as that for Brazil (28%).

Just over half of Japanese adults read weekly in 2010, whereas only 28% of Brazilians did.

Notice "that for Brazil". It stops you repeating "the percentage of adults in Brazil who read a book every week".

3. Ranking: highest, lowest and in between

  • the highest / the lowest / the largest / the smallest
  • the second highest, the third largest
  • ranked first / came last
  • the most popular / the least common
  • by far the highest (when one bar is much bigger)

Norway had the highest proportion of weekly readers in both years, while Brazil had the lowest.

In 2020, Spain moved up to second place, overtaking Japan.

4. Describing similarity

Examiners notice when you can see that two figures are close, not just different.

  • similar / roughly the same / almost identical
  • just over / just under / approximately / around
  • the same as / equal to

The figures for Brazil were similar in both years, at around 30%.

5. Describing size of difference

  • slightly / marginally (small difference)
  • considerably / significantly / substantially (large difference)
  • a gap of 15 percentage points

Japan's figure fell considerably, from 55% to 39%.

When the chart shows percentages, say percentage points for the difference. A fall from 55% to 39% is a drop of 16 percentage points, not 16%. My post on describing percentages and proportions explains this in more detail.

6. Describing change (when the chart has two or more years)

If your bar chart shows different years, you also need trend language.

  • rose, increased, grew / fell, decreased, dropped, declined
  • remained stable / stayed the same / changed very little
  • a rise of, a fall of (nouns)

The proportion of weekly readers in Spain rose from 41% to 47%.

There was a sharp drop in Japan, the only country where the figure fell by more than 10 points.

If you're not sure which verb fits the size of the change, read Increase, rise, surge, soar: which trend verbs are actually accurate?

7. Language for your overview

Your overview gives the main features without numbers. These phrases help.

  • Overall, it is clear that …
  • The most noticeable feature is …
  • In both years, …
  • The main change was …

Overall, Norway had the most regular readers in both years, and Japan saw the biggest change, with a clear fall over the period.

No numbers, one comparison and one change. That's what an overview needs to do.

Common mistakes with bar chart vocabulary

  • ✗ Norway was the highest country. ✓ Norway had the highest proportion.
  • ✗ The percentage of Spain rose. ✓ The figure for Spain rose.
  • ✗ Japan decreased 16%. ✓ The figure for Japan fell by 16 percentage points.
  • ✗ Brazil was the less. ✓ Brazil had the lowest figure.
  • ✗ More people in Norway than Spain. ✓ More people read weekly in Norway than in Spain.

The first two are easy to miss when you check your own writing. A country can't be "high"; its figure can. Always name the thing you are measuring.

Practice activities

Activity 1: Choose the right word

Use the example chart. Choose the correct word for each gap.

  1. In 2010, Norway's figure was more than twice as high ___ Brazil's. (as / than)
  2. Spain's figure rose ___ 41% ___ 47%. (from … to / at … by)
  3. Japan's figure fell ___ 16 percentage points. (by / of)
  4. Brazil had the ___ proportion of weekly readers in both years. (lower / lowest)
  5. The figures for Brazil were ___ in both years, at around 30%. (similar / same)

Answers (try first!): 1 as · 2 from … to · 3 by · 4 lowest · 5 similar

Activity 2: Upgrade the sentence

These notes are correct but too basic for Task 1. Rewrite each one as a full sentence, using a phrase from this post.

  1. Norway 62%, Brazil 28% (2010).
  2. Japan went down a lot.
  3. Spain was second in 2020.

Answers (try first!), example answers:

  1. In 2010, the figure for Norway was more than twice as high as that for Brazil.
  2. The proportion of weekly readers in Japan fell considerably, from 55% to 39%.
  3. In 2020, Spain had the second highest proportion of weekly readers.
