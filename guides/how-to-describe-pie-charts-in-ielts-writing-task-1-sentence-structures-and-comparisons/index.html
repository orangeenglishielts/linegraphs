How to Describe Pie Charts in IELTS Writing Task 1: Sentence Structures and Comparisons

By Lindsey, former IELTS examiner. 9 November 2026. Read this post with its interactive exercises.

Pie charts appear regularly in IELTS Academic Writing Task 1. A strong response does more than list percentages: it selects the main features and uses clear comparisons to explain how the parts relate to the whole.

The practice charts

Use these two fictional charts for every exercise in this lesson. They show the distribution of household energy sources in 2000 and 2020.

  • Coal: 2000: 40%; 2020: 25%
  • Gas: 2000: 25%; 2020: 25%
  • Renewable energy: 2000: 10%; 2020: 30%
  • Nuclear power: 2000: 15%; 2020: 15%
  • Oil: 2000: 10%; 2020: 5%
  • Total: 2000: 100%; 2020: 100%

The data are fictional and created for IELTS practice.

Exercise: choose the best overview

Which overview best summarises the main features of the charts?

A. Overall, coal accounted for 40% in 2000, while gas represented 25% and nuclear power made up 15%.

B. Overall, the distribution shifted away from coal and towards renewable energy. Coal was the largest source in 2000, while renewables became the largest in 2020.

C. Overall, the biggest slices were coal and renewable energy, and there were several other categories.

Best answer: B. It identifies the key change and the shift in ranking. Option A gives figures without summarising the main pattern. Option C is vague and uses informal wording.

What a pie chart asks you to describe

A pie chart shows how a whole is divided into proportions. In Task 1, focus on the relative shares, the largest and smallest categories, and any useful similarities or contrasts. Pie charts show proportions, not trends, unless the task includes charts from different years.

For charts showing completed years, describe changes using the past tense. Take care not to infer that the amount of something increased when the chart only shows its share. If energy’s share rose, for example, that does not necessarily mean the total quantity of energy produced also rose.

IELTS expects you to select and report key features, support them with accurate data and make relevant comparisons. A clear overview matters; a mechanical list of every figure does not. See the official IELTS Academic Writing test format for task requirements.

Paraphrase the question in your introduction

Use one sentence to introduce what the charts show. Keep the meaning accurate and avoid copying the task wording.

The two pie charts compare the proportions of household energy generated from five sources in 2000 and 2020.

Useful alternatives include:

  • The charts illustrate how household energy sources were distributed in 2000 and 2020.
  • The pie charts show the percentage share of five energy sources in two different years.

Use proportion, percentage or share when the figures are percentages. Choose past-tense wording for completed years.

Write an overview that identifies the main features

For these charts, the overview should capture the major shift: coal’s share fell, while renewable energy’s share rose and became the largest. It can also note that gas stayed level and oil was the smallest category in 2020.

Overall, the distribution shifted from coal towards renewable energy. Coal was the largest source in 2000, whereas renewables had become the largest by 2020.

Avoid relying on phrases such as “the biggest slice” in your formal response. It is understandable, but largest share or largest proportion is more precise and suitably academic. Also avoid putting every figure in the overview; save detailed data for the body paragraphs.

Overview-writing drill

Write a one- or two-sentence overview for the example charts. Include:

  • The biggest change.
  • The change in ranking between coal and renewables.
  • One stable or small category, if relevant.

Then check: does your overview summarise patterns rather than simply report figures?

Describe a single proportion accurately

These structures report a category’s share:

  • Coal accounted for 40% of household energy in 2000.
  • Renewable energy constituted 30% of the total in 2020.
  • Gas made up a quarter of the energy mix in both years.
  • Nuclear power represented 15% in each year.

Comprise can be used to describe what forms a whole: The energy mix comprised five sources. It is less natural as a direct substitute for accounted for in a percentage sentence.

For a fuller explanation of these verbs, fractions and proportion vocabulary, read How to Describe Percentages and Proportions in IELTS Writing Task 1. The companion lesson explores the distinctions in more detail.

Group slices into clear body paragraphs

Organise information by meaningful relationships instead of describing each chart separately. For the example, one paragraph can compare coal and renewable energy, the most notable changes. Another can cover gas, nuclear power and oil, which stayed level or had smaller shares.

A useful body structure is:

  1. Paragraph one: the largest categories or most substantial changes.
  2. Paragraph two: the remaining categories, grouped by similarity or contrast.

Not every chart will divide neatly into increases and decreases. Choose the grouping that makes the comparisons easiest to follow.

Build comparisons between categories and years

Use comparisons to make relationships clear:

  • Four times as large as: The share for coal in 2000 was four times as large as the share for renewables.
  • Six times as large as: In 2020, renewables represented six times the proportion of oil.
  • Slightly or considerably higher than: In 2000, gas’s share was considerably higher than that of renewables.
  • Compared with: Coal accounted for 40% in 2000, compared with 25% in 2020.
  • Respectively: Gas and nuclear power represented 25% and 15%, respectively, in both years.
  • While / whereas: Renewables rose from 10% to 30%, while coal fell from 40% to 25%.

Use respectively only when the order of the categories and figures is unmistakable. Avoid forcing comparisons into every sentence; prioritise those that clarify the main features.

Comparison-building exercise

Combine each pair of details into one clear sentence.

  1. Coal: 40% in 2000; 25% in 2020.
  2. Renewables: 10% in 2000; 30% in 2020.
  3. Gas: 25% in both years; nuclear power: 15% in both years.

Suggested answers

  1. Coal’s share fell from 40% in 2000 to 25% in 2020.
  2. Renewable energy’s share tripled, rising from 10% to 30%.
  3. Gas and nuclear power remained stable at 25% and 15%, respectively.

Approximate figures without inventing precision

When a chart is visually presented and exact numbers are not labelled, use careful approximation:

  • Just over a quarter
  • Just under a third
  • Roughly half
  • Approximately one fifth

Do not claim a precise figure that the chart does not support. If a slice looks close to 30%, write approximately 30% rather than assigning it an exact value without evidence. When exact percentages are provided, report them accurately.

Avoid the part–whole grammar trap

These structures describe opposite directions:

  • Part → whole: Renewable energy made up 30% of the total.
  • Whole → parts: The energy mix was made up of five sources.

A category makes up a share; a whole is made up of its components. For more examples, see the percentages and proportions lesson linked above.

Check common errors

Correct each sentence.

  1. Renewable energy made up 20 percentage points between 2000 and 2020.
  2. The energy mix comprises of five sources.
  3. Coal account for 40% of the total in 2000.
  4. Renewables rose by 20%, when the share moved from 10% to 30%.

Suggested corrections

  1. Renewable energy’s share increased by 20 percentage points.
  2. The energy mix comprises five sources.
  3. Coal accounted for 40% of the total in 2000.
  4. Renewables rose by 20 percentage points. The increase was 200% relative to the original 10% share.

A change from 10% to 30% is a 20-percentage-point increase, not a 20% increase. For more guidance on frequent Task 1 errors, read 10 Common Mistakes That Keep IELTS Writing Task 1 Scores at Band 6.

Use a 20-minute practice routine

Set a timer and write at least 150 words about the two example charts.

  • Minutes 1–3: Identify the largest shares, smallest share, key changes and stable categories.
  • Minutes 4–5: Write your introduction and overview.
  • Minutes 6–16: Develop two body paragraphs, selecting relevant figures and comparisons.
  • Minutes 17–20: Check tense, grammar, figures, percentage points and word count.

Checklist: Have you included a clear overview? Did you compare the years rather than list each chart separately? Are your figures accurate? Have you used past tense consistently for the completed years?

Practise with a study buddy

Take turns describing the charts aloud for one minute. Your partner should listen for three features: a clear overview, accurate comparisons and correct use of percentage points. Then swap roles.

Afterwards, each of you writes a short overview. Compare the summaries and discuss which one identifies the key changes most clearly. This quick routine builds both accuracy and confidence before timed practice.

Model answer

The two pie charts compare the proportions of household energy generated from five sources in 2000 and 2020.

Overall, the energy mix shifted away from coal and towards renewable sources. Coal was the largest contributor in 2000, whereas renewables had become the largest source by 2020. Oil represented the smallest share in the latter year.

Coal accounted for 40% of household energy in 2000, but its share fell to 25% by 2020, a decrease of 15 percentage points. In contrast, the share of renewable energy tripled from 10% to 30%, overtaking coal’s share to become the largest. Together, coal and renewables made up more than half of the total (55%) in 2020.

The proportion generated from gas remained unchanged at 25% in both years, while nuclear power also stayed stable, at 15%. Oil, by comparison, declined from 10% to 5%, making it the smallest category in 2020.

Why it works: The overview highlights the central shift and change in ranking. The body paragraphs group the most significant changes before reporting stable or smaller categories. Phrases such as in contrast, while and by comparison make the relationships clear, and the figures are reported without confusing percentages with percentage points.
