IELTS Academic Writing Task 1: The Complete Beginner's Guide

By Lindsey, former IELTS examiner. 6 November 2026. Read this post with its interactive exercises.

IELTS Academic Writing Task 1 becomes manageable when you know what the examiner expects. You do not need to report every number or use complicated vocabulary. You need to identify the main features, organise them clearly, and describe the visual accurately.

Warm-up game: spot the overview

Imagine a line graph showing internet use in three countries between 2000 and 2020:

  • Country A rises from 20% to 80%.
  • Country B increases from 35% to 60%.
  • Country C remains almost unchanged at around 50%.

Which sentence gives the best overview?

  1. Country A was at 20% in 2000.
  2. Internet use increased in Country A and Country B, while Country C remained stable.
  3. I think Country A developed faster because it invested more in technology.

Answer: 2. It summarises the biggest features without listing every figure or adding an opinion. This is the central skill in Task 1.

Understand exactly what Task 1 requires

In IELTS Academic Writing Task 1, you receive a graph, chart, table, map, process diagram or another visual. You must describe, summarise or explain the information in your own words.

You have approximately 20 minutes and must write at least 150 words. Task 1 is a report, not an essay. Your response should:

  • describe the information shown;
  • select the most important features;
  • make relevant comparisons;
  • use an appropriate academic or neutral style;
  • avoid personal opinions, predictions and explanations that are not shown.

The complete IELTS Academic Writing paper lasts 60 minutes and includes Task 1 and Task 2. Task 2 carries greater weight in the final Writing score, but Task 1 remains essential for achieving your target band.

Academic and General Training Task 1 are different. Academic candidates describe visual information. General Training candidates write a letter, usually formal, semi-formal or informal. The time limit and minimum word count are similar, but the task type, purpose and language are not the same. Always practise the version required by your test.

Learn how examiners award your score

Task 1 is assessed using four equally weighted criteria. From a former IELTS examiner’s perspective, the most common beginner problem is not a lack of advanced English. It is failing to complete the task clearly.

Task Achievement

This measures how fully and accurately you answer the question.

A strong response:

  • includes a clear introduction;
  • presents an overview;
  • selects the main features;
  • supports those features with accurate data;
  • makes relevant comparisons.

A response can contain accurate grammar and vocabulary but still lose marks if it simply lists numbers or omits the overview.

Coherence and Cohesion

This measures how logically your ideas are organised and connected.

The reader should understand why each sentence appears where it does. Use clear paragraphs, logical groupings and linking language. Do not force a linker into every sentence. Clear organisation is more important than repeatedly writing however, therefore and in addition.

Lexical Resource

This measures your vocabulary range, accuracy and flexibility.

You need language for trends, quantities, comparisons, stages and locations. You also need accurate collocations and word forms. Replacing every simple word with an unusual synonym is not effective. Precise, natural vocabulary achieves more than impressive but inaccurate vocabulary.

Grammatical Range and Accuracy

This measures the variety and correctness of your sentence structures.

Aim for a controlled mixture of simple, compound and complex sentences. Examiners notice verb tense, subject–verb agreement, articles, prepositions, punctuation and sentence boundaries. A few complex structures are useful, but frequent errors reduce your score.

Recognise the seven visual types

Line graph

Line graphs usually show change over time. Focus on trends, turning points, peaks, lows, stability and comparisons between lines.

Bar chart

Bar charts compare quantities across categories. Group similar or contrasting categories instead of describing every bar in isolation.

Pie chart

Pie charts show proportions, usually as percentages. Identify the largest and smallest segments and compare significant differences.

Table

Tables provide precise figures in rows and columns. Select the most important patterns rather than repeating every number.

Process diagram

A process shows stages in a sequence. Describe the order, main transformations and number of stages. Use the passive voice when the process focuses on what happens rather than who performs the action.

Map

Maps show places, layouts or changes over time. Describe major developments, such as new buildings, removed facilities, roads or changes in land use. Use location language accurately.

Multiple graphs

Multiple graphs combine two or more visuals. First identify the relationship between them. Then group information by shared categories, time periods or contrasting trends.

Use the reliable four-paragraph structure

A clear structure makes your response easier to write and easier to mark.

Introduction

Paraphrase the question in one or two sentences. Include the visual type, subject, time period and units when relevant.

Question: The line graph shows the percentage of people who used public transport in four cities between 2005 and 2020.

Introduction: The line graph illustrates the proportion of residents using public transport in four cities from 2005 to 2020.

Do not copy the question word for word.

Overview

The overview is the single highest-value paragraph for Task Achievement. State two or three major features without detailed figures.

For a graph, mention the overall direction, highest or lowest category, major contrast or unusual change. For a process, summarise the number and sequence of stages. For a map, identify the most significant developments.

Avoid numbers in the overview unless they are essential. Details belong in the body paragraphs.

Body paragraph one

Present the first logical group of details. This might include the highest figures, upward trends, early stages or one side of a map.

Body paragraph two

Present the second logical group. This might include lower figures, downward trends, later stages or contrasting changes.

The best grouping depends on the visual. A strong report does not follow a fixed order mechanically.

Build a useful language toolkit

Describe increases and decreases

Use verbs:

  • rose, increased, climbed, grew;
  • fell, decreased, declined, dropped;
  • fluctuated, peaked, remained stable, levelled off.

Use noun forms when appropriate:

  • a rise in the number of visitors;
  • a sharp decline in sales;
  • a period of stability;
  • considerable growth in demand.

Compare these structures:

  • The number of users increased sharply.
  • There was a sharp increase in the number of users.

The verb takes an adverb. The noun takes an adjective.

Show degree and speed

For small changes, use:

  • slightly;
  • marginally;
  • to a small degree;
  • modestly.

For large or fast changes, use:

  • significantly;
  • considerably;
  • sharply;
  • rapidly;
  • dramatically.

Use these words accurately. A line that moves slowly upwards shows a gradual increase, not a dramatic rise.

Use the correct prepositions

  • The figure rose from 40 to 65.
  • The figure increased by 25 units.
  • The rate stood at 40%.
  • The percentage fell to 15%.
  • There was a difference of 20 percentage points.

A common beginner error is confusing to and by. To gives the final figure; by gives the size of the change.

Make comparisons

Useful structures include:

  • X was higher than Y.
  • X was twice as high as Y.
  • X accounted for the largest proportion.
  • Y was slightly lower than X.
  • The figures for A and B were similar.
  • A and B recorded 35% and 42%, respectively.

Approximate carefully

Visual data is often approximate. Use:

  • approximately;
  • around;
  • about;
  • just over;
  • just under;
  • nearly;
  • roughly.

Do not invent precision when the graph only provides an estimate.

Control articles and word forms

Use the when referring to a specific figure or category:

  • the percentage of students;
  • the number of households;
  • the figure for transport.

Use a with a singular countable change:

  • a rise;
  • a decline;
  • a fluctuation.

Use plural nouns when referring to general categories:

  • students;
  • households;
  • vehicles.

For time-based data, choose the tense carefully. Use the past simple for completed periods in the past, the present simple for general information, and the present perfect only when the time period connects the past to the present.

Follow a 20-minute timing plan

  • Minutes 1–3: Read and analyse. Identify the visual type, topic, units, dates and categories.
  • Minutes 4–5: Find the overview. Circle the two or three biggest patterns.
  • Minutes 6–7: Plan. Decide how to group the details into two body paragraphs.
  • Minutes 8–17: Write. Produce an introduction, overview and two developed body paragraphs.
  • Minutes 18–20: Check. Confirm the word count, figures, tense, articles, prepositions, spelling and punctuation.

Aim for approximately 160–190 words. Writing far more than necessary increases the number of possible errors.

Avoid the mistakes that often cap beginners at Band 6

The most common problems are:

  1. Writing no overview or hiding it inside a long body paragraph.
  2. Listing every number without making comparisons.
  3. Adding opinions, reasons or predictions.
  4. Copying the question instead of paraphrasing it.
  5. Using the wrong tense for the time period.
  6. Confusing percentages, units and percentage points.
  7. Repeating increase, decrease, good and bad without precision.
  8. Using memorised sentences that do not fit the visual.
  9. Writing fewer than 150 words.
  10. Ignoring articles, prepositions and subject–verb agreement.

Practise with a friend or study buddy

Use a simple three-round routine.

Round one: Overview race. Give both learners the same graph. Allow two minutes to write one overview sentence. Compare the sentences and identify which one covers the most important features.

Round two: Grouping challenge. Each person chooses two logical groups for the body paragraphs. Explain why the grouping works. Reject groupings that simply follow the order of the visual without a clear purpose.

Round three: Examiner feedback. Exchange reports. Your partner checks four points:

  • Is there a clear overview?
  • Are the paragraphs logically organised?
  • Are the comparisons accurate?
  • Which three grammar or vocabulary errors appear most often?

Do not correct every sentence immediately. First identify the pattern. If you repeatedly write increased of, for example, practise the structure increased by and increased to separately.

Practice activity: the Task 1 data detective

Choose a graph about a high-frequency IELTS topic such as education, transport, energy, health, employment or population. Before writing, complete these prompts:

  • The overall trend is…
  • The highest category is…
  • The lowest category is…
  • The most noticeable change is…
  • A useful comparison is…
  • The best body paragraph grouping is…

Then write your report in 20 minutes. Highlight every number and check that each one supports a main feature rather than appearing as an isolated detail.

Quick quiz

Choose the best answer.

1. What is the purpose of the overview?

A. To list every figure

B. To give your opinion

C. To summarise the main features

D. To explain why the data changed

2. Which sentence is correct?

A. The figure increased of 20 to 40.

B. The figure increased by 20, from 20 to 40.

C. The figure increased at 20 to 40.

D. The figure increased in 20 by 40.

3. What should you do with a process diagram?

A. Explain your opinion about the process

B. Describe the stages in order

C. Compare it with another process

D. Predict what will happen next

4. Which criterion focuses on paragraphing and logical organisation?

A. Task Achievement

B. Coherence and Cohesion

C. Lexical Resource

D. Grammatical Range and Accuracy

Answers: 1C, 2B, 3B, 4B.
