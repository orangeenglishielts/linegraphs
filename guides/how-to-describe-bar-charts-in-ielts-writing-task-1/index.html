How to Describe Bar Charts in IELTS Writing Task 1

By Lindsey, former IELTS examiner. 9 November 2026. Read this post with its interactive exercises.

Bar charts appear regularly in IELTS Academic Writing Task 1. To describe them well, you need to identify the main features, group related information and make accurate comparisons, not report every bar one by one.

This guide to how to describe bar charts in IELTS includes a clear structure, practical IELTS bar chart vocabulary, exercises and a timed writing task. The sample figures below are fictional and created for practice.

Read the chart before you write

A bar chart presents categories and quantities. The categories might be cities, age groups, transport methods or household expenses. The quantities could be numbers, percentages, money, distances or another unit.

First, check the axes and the chart title. In particular, look at the x-axis: it may show categories, time periods or both. A chart showing one year is mainly about comparing categories. A chart showing several years also requires trend language.

A static chart calls for language such as the highest, the lowest and similar to. A time-based chart needs language such as increased, fell and remained stable. Some charts combine both: describe the change over time and compare the categories.

Before reading on, choose the best overview:

A. Northbridge consumed 14 cups per person, Eastford 12, Westhaven 9, Southport 7 and Hilltown 4.

B. Overall, coffee consumption was highest in Northbridge and lowest in Hilltown, while the three other cities recorded intermediate figures.

C. The chart shows the number of cups of coffee in five cities.

Best answer: B. It summarises the main pattern without listing numbers. A reports every figure, while C introduces the topic but does not summarise the main features.

Use a simple bar chart IELTS Task 1 structure

A dependable bar chart IELTS Task 1 structure has four parts:

  1. Introduction: Paraphrase what the chart shows.
  2. Overview: Summarise the most noticeable features, without specific figures.
  3. Body paragraph one: Describe one logical group of categories with supporting data.
  4. Body paragraph two: Describe the remaining group and make relevant comparisons.

Task 1 asks you to summarise the visual information, select and report the main features, and make comparisons where relevant. Write at least 150 words. For more on the overall task and its requirements, see IELTS Academic Writing Task 1 preparation and the official IELTS writing resources.

Write a precise introduction

Name the visual, subject and relevant unit. For example:

The bar chart compares the average weekly coffee consumption per person in five cities, measured in cups.

Avoid copying the question word for word. Do not add background information that is not shown in the chart.

Give a clear overview

Choose the two or three features that matter most. For the sample chart, these are the highest and lowest figures and the general spread between the cities:

Overall, Northbridge recorded the highest coffee consumption, whereas Hilltown had the lowest. The other three cities fell between these extremes.

Save exact figures for the body paragraphs. An overview is a summary, not a data list.

Group bars to make your answer easier to follow

Organise your body paragraphs by pattern, not automatically in the order the bars appear. You could group the highest categories together, compare similar values, or separate categories with notably higher and lower figures.

For this chart, one sensible plan is to describe Northbridge and Eastford together, then compare Westhaven, Southport and Hilltown. Another option is to organise the figures from highest to lowest. Both approaches work if the grouping is clear and the important data is covered.

Grouping drill: Sort these values into two groups: 14, 12, 9, 7, 4. Which two are highest? Which three form the lower group?

Suggested grouping:

Northbridge (14) and Eastford (12) form the higher group. Westhaven (9), Southport (7) and Hilltown (4) form the lower group. Other groupings are possible if your comparisons remain clear.

Build accurate comparisons

Useful comparing categories IELTS bar chart phrases include:

  • Ranking: Northbridge recorded the highest figure; Hilltown had the lowest.
  • Relative size: Northbridge’s figure was twice that of Southport.
  • Multiples: Northbridge recorded three and a half times the figure for Hilltown.
  • Difference: Coffee consumption in Westhaven was slightly higher than in Southport.
  • Contrast: Eastford stood at 12 cups, compared with 9 in Westhaven.
  • Paired figures: The figures for Northbridge and Eastford were 14 and 12 cups, respectively.

Use twice as high as only when the values support that exact comparison. Here, 14 is twice 7, so Northbridge’s figure was twice Southport’s. Use slightly higher for a small difference and considerably higher for a larger one.

Comparison exercise: Complete the sentences using the chart.

  1. Northbridge’s figure was ______ that of Southport.
  2. Eastford recorded ______ cups per person than Westhaven.
  3. Northbridge consumed ______ the amount recorded in Hilltown.

Suggested answers:

  1. Twice
  2. Three more
  3. Three and a half times

Describe individual figures and round carefully

Useful verbs in IELTS bar chart vocabulary include:

  • stands at for a figure on a chart with no past time period: “The figure for Westhaven stands at 9 cups.”
  • recorded for a past period: “Eastford recorded 12 cups per person.”
  • reached when describing a value attained over time: “The figure reached 14 cups in 2025.”
  • accounted for when a figure represents a share of a total: “Housing accounted for 40% of expenditure.”

Choose the verb that fits the chart and its unit. For more ways to describe proportions, see Describing Percentages and Proportions in IELTS Writing Task 1.

When bars are presented visually, exact values may not be labelled. Estimate and round sensibly, about 12 or approximately 12, rather than inventing precision such as 12.37. If the chart gives exact data, report it accurately.

Keep static and time-based language separate

A chart with categories but no time progression is static. Describe rankings and differences: was higher than, recorded the lowest figure, was similar to.

A chart comparing several time periods is dynamic. Describe changes: rose from, fell to, remained stable or increased over the period. Use the correct tense for the dates shown. Check that you describe both the trend and the category comparisons.

For practice with time-based visuals, follow this lesson alongside Orange English’s line graph content. Bar charts with dates share some trend language with line graphs, but always describe the visual that appears in your task.

Avoid three common bar-chart traps

  1. Describing every bar in isolation: This creates a list instead of a summary. Group categories and highlight meaningful comparisons.
  2. Inventing precision: Do not report decimal places the chart does not provide. Estimate and round only when the visual requires it.
  3. Confusing percentages with units: If a chart measures cups, people, kilometres or dollars, use that unit. Do not describe a value as a percentage unless the chart gives percentages or the calculation is clear.

Also check subject–verb agreement and comparison grammar: The figure stands at 12, but the figures stand at 12 and 9.

Error-spotting: Correct the mistakes in these sentences.

  1. Northbridge record the highest figure.
  2. Consumption rose to 3 cups from 7 cups.
  3. Westhaven accounted for 9% of coffee consumption. (The chart measures cups per person, not percentages.)

Suggested corrections:

  1. Northbridge records the highest figure.
  2. Consumption fell by 4 cups, from 7 to 3. Use to for the ending value and by for the size of the change.
  3. Westhaven recorded 9 cups per person per week.

Complete a 20-minute practice task

Write at least 150 words summarising the coffee chart. Use 20 minutes, as recommended for IELTS Writing Task 1.

  • Minutes 1–3: Check the title, axes, units and whether the chart shows time.
  • Minutes 4–5: Note the highest and lowest categories; choose two logical groups.
  • Minutes 6–16: Write an introduction, overview and two body paragraphs.
  • Minutes 17–20: Check your comparisons, units, verb agreement and word count.

Model answer

The bar chart compares average weekly coffee consumption per person in five fictional cities, measured in cups.

Overall, Northbridge recorded the highest consumption, while Hilltown had the lowest. The figures for Eastford and Westhaven were also relatively high, whereas Southport and Hilltown recorded lower amounts.

Northbridge residents consumed 14 cups of coffee per person each week, the highest figure shown. Eastford followed at 12 cups, two fewer than Northbridge. Westhaven’s average was 9 cups, placing it between the two leading cities and the lower-consumption group.

Southport residents consumed 7 cups per week, which was half the Northbridge figure. Hilltown had the lowest consumption, at 4 cups per person. Northbridge’s average was therefore three and a half times that of Hilltown, while Westhaven recorded five more cups than Hilltown. Eastford’s figure was exactly three times the Hilltown average, while Southport consumed only two cups fewer than Westhaven, so the gap between these two cities was relatively narrow.

This illustrative model demonstrates an overview, logical grouping and accurate comparisons. A particular score depends on the full assessment criteria.

Language focus: recorded the highest identifies a ranking; followed at varies the reporting verb; two fewer than makes a direct comparison; and at 4 cups per person gives the unit clearly.

Practise with a study buddy

Take turns describing the sample chart aloud for one minute. Your partner should note the overview, the groups you chose and one comparison you made. Then switch roles. Together, check whether each figure includes its unit and whether any comparison overstates what the chart shows.
