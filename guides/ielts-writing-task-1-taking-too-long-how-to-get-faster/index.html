IELTS Writing Task 1 taking too long? How to get faster

By Lindsey, former IELTS examiner. 3 November 2026. Read this post with its interactive exercises.

If Task 1 is taking you 30 minutes or more, start by looking at what you’re trying to include.

The instructions ask you to select and report the main features, making comparisons where relevant. That word select matters. You don’t need to describe every data point or compare everything with everything else.

As an IELTS teacher and former examiner, I work on two things with students who find this task slow: choosing what to write about and becoming more fluent at writing it.

Choose comparisons that mean something

Look for the things that jump out at you. Very high figures, very low figures, obvious similarities and differences. For graphs showing change over time, look at the main patterns too.

Imagine a chart comparing holiday activities among American and Chinese respondents. You could identify the most and least popular activities in each group. You might also notice an activity that is equally popular with both.

But comparing the percentage of Chinese respondents who choose hiking with the percentage of American respondents who choose sightseeing might not tell the reader anything useful. You’ve changed both the group and the activity. Unless there’s a particular reason to connect those figures, why compare them?

Don’t compare apples and oranges just because both numbers are there.

You can leave some details out. The aim is to give the reader a clear picture of the main features, supported by relevant figures.

Practise the introduction and overview separately

You don’t always need to write a complete response to practise Task 1.

With my students, I often set introductions and overviews as homework. At first, the work might be scaffolded, with gaps to fill. Then students move on to writing their own.

A useful sequence would be ten introductions, ten overviews and then ten complete responses, using different questions. Ten isn’t a magic number. The point is to repeat the skill enough for it to become familiar.

For the introduction, that means getting used to paraphrasing the prompt accurately. For the overview, it means looking at a new chart and deciding what the main message is.

The language can start to feel like second nature. You still need to look carefully at each graph, but you spend less time wondering how to begin.

The body paragraphs depend more on the particular data. Becoming comfortable with introductions and overviews leaves you more time and attention for those decisions.

Notice the language that keeps slowing you down

Prepositions, tenses and articles are common problems in this practice.

For example, we write “from 2010 to 2020” or “between 2010 and 2020”. If you’re unsure about these patterns every time you write, they interrupt your flow.

Check the dates before choosing your tense. Past dates usually need past-tense descriptions. When a chart has no dates, the present simple is often appropriate, depending on the context.

Different visuals also call for different language. Describing changes in a line graph isn’t quite the same as comparing categories in a bar chart or explaining the stages of a process. Practise the vocabulary and grammar you need for the visual in front of you.

One phrase I encourage students to avoid is “the chart below” or “the chart above”. Those words refer to the layout of the question. Your description doesn’t need them.

“The chart illustrates…” or “The chart compares…” is enough. Choose a verb that fits what the chart shows.

Take your time before you start timing yourself

Initially, I let students take as long as they need.

They’re learning to read the chart, select features, make comparisons and use the language accurately. I want them to become comfortable with that process.

Once it feels more familiar, we introduce timed practice and work towards the recommended 20 minutes for Task 1.

For your next practice session, try writing just an introduction and an overview. Check them, notice the errors you repeat, and try a different chart. Give yourself time to learn the skill before expecting yourself to do it quickly.

Practice: a scaffolded introduction and overview

Here’s that holiday chart, so you can start now. Take as long as you need. The figures are invented for practice.

The task: The chart below shows the percentage of American and Chinese respondents who choose five holiday activities. Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features, and make comparisons where relevant.

Exercise 1: Complete the introduction

Fill each gap with a word from the box. Notice that the chart has no dates, so the sentence uses the present simple.

Word box: compares / proportion / select

The bar chart (1) ______ the (2) ______ of people from the USA and China who (3) ______ five different holiday activities.

Answers:

(1) compares (2) proportion (3) select

Exercise 2: Choose what to compare

Which comparison would you leave out?

A. Beach holidays are far more popular with American respondents (62%) than Chinese respondents (28%).

B. 19% of Chinese respondents choose hiking, while 55% of American respondents choose sightseeing.

C. Museums attract the same proportion of both groups (45%).

D. Shopping shows a wide gap, at 22% of American respondents and 64% of Chinese respondents.

Best answer: B. It changes both the group and the activity, so the two figures tell the reader very little.

Exercise 3: Complete the overview

Fill each gap with a word or phrase from the box. Your overview gives the main message, so it doesn’t need figures.

Word box: beach holidays / hiking / least / museums / sightseeing

Overall, (1) ______ are the most popular choice among American respondents, whereas (2) ______ is the favourite among Chinese respondents. The (3) ______ popular activity is shopping for Americans and (4) ______ for Chinese respondents, while (5) ______ attract the same proportion of both groups.

Answers:

(1) beach holidays (2) sightseeing (3) least (4) hiking (5) museums

Exercise 4: Write your own

Now close the exercises above and write your own introduction and overview for the same chart, with no gaps. Before you check, ask yourself:

  • Have I paraphrased the prompt accurately?
  • Have I avoided “the chart below”?
  • Have I used the present simple?
  • Does my overview give the main message without listing figures?

Model answer:

The bar chart compares the popularity of five holiday activities among people from the USA and China.

Overall, American respondents are most likely to choose beach holidays, whereas sightseeing is the top choice for Chinese respondents. The widest gap between the two groups is in shopping, while museums are equally popular with both.
