What an IELTS Examiner Actually Looks For in Writing Task 1

By Lindsey, former IELTS examiner. 9 November 2026. Read this post with its interactive exercises.

Before you read on, try this quick warm-up. Imagine an IELTS Task 1 answer begins:

The graph shows the number of visitors to three museums from 2010 to 2020. Overall, visits to the City Museum rose, while the other two museums saw little change.

What might an examiner notice first on a quick read?

  • The overview identifies the main trends.
  • The report uses “shows” rather than a more advanced verb.
  • The opening sentence has a simple structure.
  • The answer may need more detailed comparisons.

Answer: The overview and the way the report is organised are likely to stand out first. That does not mean one sentence decides the whole band: IELTS Writing Task 1 is assessed against four equally weighted criteria. But a clear overview gives the examiner an important signal that you have understood and selected the key information.

If you are self-studying and stuck at Band 6–6.5, knowing what IELTS examiners look for can help you practise with purpose. Here is the examiner perspective on writing Task 1, translated into decisions you can make on the page.

How is IELTS Task 1 marked?

In Academic Writing Task 1, the examiner assesses your response using four criteria:

  1. Task Achievement: Have you selected and reported the key information?
  2. Coherence and Cohesion: Is your report organised and easy to follow?
  3. Lexical Resource: Is your vocabulary accurate, natural and flexible?
  4. Grammatical Range and Accuracy: Do you use varied sentence structures accurately?

Each criterion contributes equally to the Writing Task 1 score. The examiner uses the official band descriptors, not a comparison with another candidate’s answer. You can read the official IELTS Writing band descriptors.

The criteria apply to different parts of your answer, but they work together. A precise verb cannot compensate for a missing overview. A complex sentence will not help if it makes the data hard to understand.

The four criteria, translated into practical decisions

Task Achievement: select, compare and summarise

The examiner checks whether you have answered the task accurately and highlighted its main features. For most charts and graphs, a clear overview is essential. It should summarise the most important trends, differences or changes without listing every figure.

In the detail paragraphs, support that overview with relevant data and comparisons. Avoid describing every point in order or adding explanations that the visual does not provide.

On your page: write an overview that names the main pattern, then choose figures that demonstrate it.

Coherence and Cohesion: make the report easy to follow

The examiner looks at how your information is organised and connected. A clear introduction, a separate overview and logically grouped detail paragraphs help the reader follow your report.

Linking words can support progression, but they cannot create it on their own. Repeating “however”, “moreover” and “therefore” at the start of every sentence can make the writing feel mechanical.

On your page: group related trends or categories together, and use linking expressions only when they show a real relationship.

Lexical Resource: choose precise, natural language

Vocabulary is assessed for range, accuracy and appropriacy. The goal is not to sound impressive; it is to describe the data precisely. Use trend language that matches the size and direction of a change, and choose natural combinations such as “rose steadily” or “remained stable”.

For example, “surged” suggests a sharp rise. It would overstate a small, gradual increase. Flexible vocabulary helps, but forced synonyms can make a report less accurate.

On your page: prioritise the right word over a rare word. For extra practice with proportions, see Describing Percentages and Proportions in IELTS Writing Task 1.

Grammatical Range and Accuracy: vary structures with control

The examiner looks for a suitable range of sentence structures as well as accuracy. Complex sentences can help you compare trends or combine related information, but complexity is not a goal in itself. A correct, clear sentence is stronger than a long sentence that loses control of its grammar.

Choose tense according to the information in the visual. Check articles, plurals, subject–verb agreement and punctuation as well as sentence variety.

On your page: use different structures where they make a comparison or relationship clearer. The companion guide Complex Sentences in IELTS Writing explores ways to do this accurately.

Read a sample through an examiner’s lens

Imagine a line graph showing the number of visitors to three museums from 2010 to 2020. The City Museum rises from 20,000 to 60,000 visitors. The History Museum remains close to 40,000, while the Art Museum falls from 50,000 to 30,000.

The line graph compares visitor numbers at three museums between 2010 and 2020. Overall, attendance at the City Museum increased substantially, while the Art Museum experienced a decline. The History Museum’s figures remained relatively stable throughout the period. In 2010, the Art Museum was the most popular, with 50,000 visitors, compared with 40,000 at the History Museum and 20,000 at the City Museum. The City Museum’s total then climbed steadily, reaching 60,000 by 2020. By contrast, visitor numbers at the Art Museum fell gradually to 30,000. The History Museum fluctuated only slightly around 40,000 and finished the period at approximately its starting level.

Examiner-style notes

  • Task Achievement: The overview highlights the main movements. The selected figures support it; the report does not list every imagined data point.
  • Coherence and Cohesion: The introduction, overview and detail are easy to identify. “By contrast” signals a useful comparison.
  • Lexical Resource: “Climbed steadily”, “fell gradually” and “fluctuated” describe different patterns. Each word must still match the graph.
  • Grammatical Range and Accuracy: The report uses a mix of sentence forms and comparative language. Accuracy matters more than adding complexity for its own sake.

A sample like this is a guide to the decisions involved, not a guaranteed band score. The actual mark depends on the full response and the official descriptors.

What examiners notice during a first read

A response is read as a whole, not marked by hunting for a magic phrase. On a quick first pass, an examiner can get a sense of whether the report has a recognisable structure and whether the overview captures the main features. A clear paragraph structure makes the information easier to follow; it does not earn a separate bonus before grammar is considered.

The examiner then assesses the response against each criterion. Memorised phrases do not create an automatic penalty, but they are often easy to spot when they do not fit the visual or the task. A pre-learned introduction that says little about the actual chart cannot replace accurate selection and comparison.

At a glance, a Band 6 response may cover some key features and attempt an overview, yet remain general, repetitive or unevenly controlled. A response closer to Band 7 tends to make the main features clearer, organise information more effectively and use a wider range of language with generally good control. These are broad patterns, not a scoring shortcut: the descriptors decide the band.

Practise identifying the criterion

Match each sentence to the criterion it most directly affects: Task Achievement, Coherence and Cohesion, Lexical Resource, or Grammatical Range and Accuracy.

  1. “Overall, the two figures are different.”
  2. “Moreover, the number rose. Moreover, it then fell.”
  3. “The figure skyrocketed dramatically from 41 to 43.”
  4. “The percentage of visitors increase steadily.”

Show answers

  1. Task Achievement: the overview is too vague to identify the main pattern.
  2. Coherence and Cohesion: the same linker is repeated mechanically and does not show how the ideas relate.
  3. Lexical Resource: “skyrocketed dramatically” exaggerates a very small change.
  4. Grammatical Range and Accuracy. The verb should agree with “percentage”: “increases” for present-tense data, or “increased” for a finished past period.

Run this self-audit on your next report

After writing a practice answer, check each point:

  • Have I included a clear overview of the main trends or differences?
  • Have I selected key details instead of listing every figure?
  • Have I grouped related information into clear paragraphs?
  • Are my comparisons and numbers accurate?
  • Does each trend verb match the scale of the change?
  • Have I used a range of sentence structures without sacrificing accuracy?
  • Have I checked tense, articles, plurals and subject–verb agreement?

A “no” answer gives you a specific next step. If the overview is weak, practise summarising the visual before drafting the detail paragraphs. If the language is inaccurate, edit for precision before adding more advanced vocabulary.

Practise with a study buddy

Swap Task 1 reports with a friend. One person plays the examiner and uses the four criteria as a checklist. They should give a band impression only if they can explain it with evidence from the writing: for example, “The overview is clear, but the detail is mostly listed rather than compared.”

Then switch roles. The goal is not to guess an exact score; it is to connect feedback to a criterion and choose one improvement for the next practice task.
