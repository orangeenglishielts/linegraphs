IELTS Task 1 Maps: Examples, Grammar and Practice

By Lindsey, former IELTS examiner. 6 November 2026. Read this post with its interactive exercises.

Maps can look intimidating in IELTS Academic Writing Task 1. But once you know what to look for, and you have some useful language for describing changes, the task becomes much more manageable. As a former IELTS examiner, I’d start by noticing what has changed before you think about impressive vocabulary.

Read each section, try the short exercises and then finish by writing your own response. All the maps and practice questions here are original and fictional, not official IELTS test questions.

First, meet Westford

The maps show the same area in 1990 and today. Keep coming back to them as you work through this post. What has changed? What has stayed the same?

1. Start with the dates

Before you write anything, check the time periods. Are you comparing two points in the past, the past with the present, or the present with a future plan? That helps you choose your tenses.

  • Two past dates: “Between 1990 and 2010, the factory was replaced by housing.” The past simple often does the job.
  • Looking back from a past date: “By 2010, the factory had been replaced.” The past perfect emphasises completion by that point.
  • Past to now: “The factory has been replaced by a supermarket.” The present perfect links the past to now. You can also contrast the two times: “In 1990, there was a factory here, but today there is a supermarket.”
  • A future proposal: “A new bridge is planned” or “A bridge will be built.” Use the future perfect when completion by a future point matters: “By 2030, the bridge will have been built.”

Two dates don’t automatically require a perfect tense. Choose the form that expresses the meaning you need.

Try it: choose the tense

These are separate grammar examples, not descriptions of Westford.

  1. Between 1990 and 2010, the factory was demolished / has been demolished.
  2. Since 2005, several houses were built / have been built.
  3. A proposal for next year shows that a bridge will be constructed / was constructed.
  4. By 2020, the farmland had been replaced / has been replaced by housing.

Answers:

  1. Was demolished: a finished past period.
  2. Have been built: “since” connects a past starting point to now.
  3. Will be constructed: a future development.
  4. Had been replaced: completion viewed from a past point.

2. Introduce the maps, then give the big picture

For the introduction, paraphrase the question and identify the place and time periods. Keep it straightforward:

The maps show how Westford has changed since 1990.

Your overview should explain the main changes. Has the area become more residential, commercial or industrial? Has farmland disappeared? Has it become more developed for tourism?

Only use descriptions the maps support. Don’t assume that new buildings mean a place is richer or has more people. If there is no single overall transformation, summarise the biggest changes instead.

Overall, Westford has become less agricultural and more residential, while its transport infrastructure has expanded. The school has remained unchanged.

Try it: choose the strongest overview

Use the Westford maps. Which option best summarises the main changes?

A. Overall, Westford has changed considerably since 1990.

B. Overall, Westford has become less agricultural and more residential, with improvements to its transport infrastructure, while the school has remained unchanged.

C. Overall, there is a supermarket in the bottom left corner and a school near the river.

Best answer: B. It identifies the main transformation, the transport changes and an unchanged feature. A is too general, and C lists locations without summarising the changes.

3. Group your details logically

There is no single paragraph structure for every map. You can group changes by location or by type, such as transport, housing and other buildings. Choose the grouping that makes your comparisons easiest to follow.

Signposting can help: “With regard to transport…” or “In terms of changes to buildings and land use…”. Use these phrases when they introduce a clear category, rather than adding one to every sentence.

You don’t have to describe every small detail. Prioritise the biggest additions, removals and replacements, and include significant features that have stayed the same.

Try it: plan your paragraphs

Sort these Westford features into two groups, buildings and land use and transport. Then write an opening sentence for the transport paragraph.

  • Housing replacing farmland
  • A supermarket replacing the factory
  • The unchanged school
  • The wider main road
  • The new bridge

Possible answers:

Buildings and land use: housing, supermarket, school. Transport: road, bridge.

“With regard to transport, the main road has been widened and a bridge has been built across the river.”

Other groupings can work if they have a clear logic, including grouping by location.

4. Build up your passive grammar

The maps usually show what happened, without telling us who did it. “Someone built a bridge” is grammatical, but “A bridge was built” focuses on the information shown.

Start with was/were + past participle. Then add perfect forms when the time frame calls for them: has/have been + past participle.

  • The bridge has been built.
  • The houses have been built.

Many useful map verbs are regular: constructed, expanded, extended, developed, redeveloped and demolished. Learn irregular forms such as build → built, too.

Not every sentence needs a passive. “The town expanded”, “A bridge crosses the river” and “The school remains in the same location” are all useful active sentences. “Become” has no passive form, so “was became” is always wrong.

Try it: spot the mistakes

Correct each sentence. These are grammar examples.

  1. A new hotel have been constructed.
  2. Several houses were build beside the river.
  3. The town was became more residential.
  4. The road is now more wide than before.
  5. The largest development is in centre of the town.
  6. New housings have been built near the station.

Answers:

  1. A new hotel has been constructed. The subject is singular.
  2. Several houses were built beside the river. You need the past participle.
  3. The town became more residential. There is no passive here.
  4. The road is now wider than before. This is the comparative form.
  5. The largest development is in the centre of the town.
  6. New housing has been built near the station. “Housing” is uncountable. “New houses have been built” also works.

5. Practise describing locations

Start with a simple map and describe it aloud. Useful phrases include “in the top left corner”, “in the centre of the town”, “next to”, “adjacent to” and “opposite”. Be precise: use “opposite” when two features face each other across a road or space.

The Westford maps include a north arrow, so you can use compass directions. Without one, top, bottom, left and right are safer choices than assuming north is at the top.

  • In the north of the town: inside its northern part.
  • To the north of the town: outside the town, further north.

For Westford, you could write: “The school is east of the housing and west of the river.”

6. Use the language you know

If you can’t remember “widened”, write “The road is now wider than before”. A clear, accurate sentence is useful even if it’s simple.

Learn vocabulary in short phrases: a building was demolished; farmland was replaced by housing; a road was widened; a bridge crosses a river. “Derelict” means abandoned and in poor condition. Only use it when the map gives you that information, not just because a building is old.

Try it: describe the change another way

These sentences compare the past with the present. Rewrite them using the word in brackets.

  1. The car park was enlarged. (larger)
  2. The road was narrowed. (narrower)
  3. The town became more residential. (housing)

Possible answers:

  1. The car park is now larger than before.
  2. The road is now narrower than before.
  3. There is now more housing in the town.

Activity: you’re the IELTS editor

Now bring the skills together. Use the Westford maps to help a student improve their response. Allow about 10 to 15 minutes, and use a notebook or document for your answers.

Step 1: notice and select

List three changes you would prioritise, then one feature that has stayed the same.

Possible answers:

There is no single correct set of three, but the change from farmland to housing is particularly important for the overall picture.

Step 2: improve the overview

Overall, there are some changes. There are houses, a supermarket and a bridge.

What is missing from this overview?

A. More difficult vocabulary

B. A clear overall transformation

C. The exact location of every building

Best answer: B. Listing buildings is not the same as explaining how the area has changed.

Now complete it: “Overall, Westford has become more ________, with ________ replacing the farmland.”

Answers: residential, housing

Step 3: repair the grammar

Two sentences need correcting. One is already correct.

  1. Several new houses has been build.
  2. The road has become more wide.
  3. A supermarket has replaced the factory, while the school has remained unchanged.

Answers:

  1. Several new houses have been built.
  2. The road has become wider.
  3. Correct. It contrasts a change with an unchanged feature.

Step 4: make an organisational decision

Create two paragraph headings and assign the housing, supermarket, school, road and bridge to them. Explain in one sentence why your grouping works.

Possible answers:

Buildings and land use: housing, supermarket, school. Transport: road, bridge.

This keeps related changes together and lets you compare an unchanged building with the changed ones. Another logical grouping is also acceptable.

Step 5: write a short paragraph

Write two or three sentences comparing transport in 1990 with transport today. You could start with “With regard to transport…”, “The road is now…” or “A new bridge has…”.

Possible answers:

With regard to transport, the main road is now wider than it was in 1990. A new bridge has also been built across the river, allowing the road to continue to the eastern side.

Your turn: write the whole response

The task: The maps show Westford in 1990 and today. Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features, and make comparisons where relevant. Write at least 150 words.

This is an original practice task. Take your time on your first attempt. Once you’re more comfortable selecting details and using the language, practise completing the task in about 20 minutes.

Before you finish

  • Have you introduced the maps and included a clear overview?
  • Are the detail paragraphs grouped logically?
  • Have you compared the maps directly?
  • Do your tenses match the dates?
  • Are your past participles, passive forms and subject-verb agreement correct?
  • Are your comparatives correct, and have you included “the” with superlatives where needed?
  • Have you checked countable and uncountable nouns, such as “houses” and “housing”?

Model answer

Read this after writing your own. It’s an illustrative answer, not an officially scored response.

Model answer:

The maps show how Westford has changed since 1990.

Overall, the area has become less agricultural and more residential, with housing replacing farmland. Transport infrastructure has also expanded, while the school and the course of the river have remained unchanged.

In terms of buildings and land use, the farmland in the north-west of the area has been replaced by six houses. To the east of this development, the school still occupies the same site on the western side of the river. Further south, the factory that stood below the main road in 1990 has been replaced by a supermarket in the same location.

With regard to transport, the main road has been widened. In 1990, it ran from the western edge of the area towards the river but stopped at the bank. Today, a new bridge carries the road across the river, and the road continues to the eastern edge of the map.

Why this works: paragraph 1 introduces the maps, paragraph 2 gives the overview, paragraph 3 groups buildings and land use, and paragraph 4 groups transport. The response compares the maps directly and makes no unsupported claims about population or wealth.
