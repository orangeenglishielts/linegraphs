How to Describe a Line Graph in 20 Minutes: A Step-by-Step Plan

By Lindsey, former IELTS examiner. 6 November 2026. Read this post with its interactive exercises.

Many candidates lose marks before they write their first sentence. They begin drafting while they are still working out what the graph shows. Then they spot a major trend halfway through a paragraph, change direction and run short of time.

Warm-up quiz: Where do most candidates lose marks in the first five minutes?

A. They do not use enough advanced vocabulary.

B. They start writing before they understand the graph.

C. They spend too long checking punctuation.

Best answer: B. Reading and planning are not wasted time; they help you select the features your response needs.

This guide gives you a practical answer to how to describe a line graph IELTS candidates can use under exam conditions. It follows a clear IELTS Writing Task 1 line graph 20 minutes plan, from decoding the chart to checking your final response. Follow this IELTS Task 1 time-management plan

Minutes 0–2: Read and decode

Before writing, check the graph’s:

  • Title: What subject is being measured?
  • Axes: What do the horizontal and vertical axes show?
  • Units: Are the figures percentages, numbers, money or something else?
  • Time span: Does it show a completed period, the present or projections?
  • Categories: How many lines are there, and what does each represent?

Decode exercise: Imagine a graph titled Commuter numbers by transport type, 1990–2020. The vertical axis measures commuters in thousands. It has lines for bus, car and bicycle use. List the five checks above before you write.

Show answer: Subject: commuter numbers by transport type. Horizontal axis: years from 1990 to 2020. Vertical axis: commuters in thousands. Time span: 1990–2020. Categories: bus, car and bicycle. Note the units: these are thousands of commuters, not percentages.

Minutes 2–4: Find the main features

Look for the highest and lowest points, the biggest change, and the overall trends. Note these as short phrases, not full sentences. You do not need to describe every point; choose the features that give the reader the clearest picture.

Main-features drill: In the fictional commuter graph above, bicycle use rises from 20,000 to 70,000. Bus use falls from 60,000 to 35,000. Car use stays close to 40,000 throughout. Which two main features belong in the overview?

Show answer: Bicycle use rises substantially, while bus use declines. Car use remains comparatively stable. The overview should summarise these broad patterns. Save the exact figures for the detail paragraphs.

Minutes 4–5: Write the introduction

Paraphrase the question in one sentence. State what the graph shows and include its time period and units where relevant. Replace shows with an accurate alternative such as illustrates, compares or presents.

The line graph compares the numbers of commuters travelling by bus, car and bicycle between 1990 and 2020.

Keep the introduction brief. Its job is to introduce the visual, not interpret every trend.

Minutes 5–7: Write the overview

Write two sentences that summarise the most important patterns. Avoid detailed figures here; use them later to support your comparisons.

Overall, bicycle use increased markedly, while bus use followed a downward trend. Car use remained relatively stable throughout the period.

A clear overview is essential: it shows that you have understood the main message of the graph rather than simply copied data into sentences. For more guidance on selecting key features, see the official IELTS Writing assessment criteria.

Minutes 7–17: Write two detail paragraphs

Group information logically. For example, describe the biggest changes together in one paragraph, then cover steadier data in another. Start each paragraph with its main comparison and support it with a few accurate figures.

Bicycle use rose from 20,000 commuters in 1990 to 45,000 in 2010, before reaching 70,000 in 2020. By contrast, bus use declined steadily, from 60,000 to 45,000 and then 35,000. The two were level at 45,000 in 2010, after which cycling moved ahead.
Car use changed very little. The figure stood at 40,000 in 1990, rose slightly to 42,000 in 2000 and ended at approximately 41,000.

These figures are illustrative data, created for this example. The key technique is to compare related trends directly instead of listing every value year by year.

Minutes 17–20: Check the response

Use the final minutes to check:

  • Tense: Does it match the time period? Use the past simple for completed periods.
  • Figures and units: Have you reported values accurately, with the correct units?
  • Grammar: Check subject–verb agreement, articles, plurals and sentence punctuation.
  • Overview: Is it included and free of detailed figures?
  • Word count: Is your answer above 150 words?
  • Relevance: Have you avoided opinions or explanations that the graph does not provide?

See the plan in action

Here is the full line graph IELTS step by step method applied to our fictional commuter data:

  • Read and decode: commuter numbers; bus, car and bicycle; 1990–2020; figures in thousands.
  • Find the main features: bicycle use rises sharply; bus use falls; car use stays steady.
  • Introduction: The line graph compares the numbers of commuters travelling by bus, car and bicycle between 1990 and 2020.
  • Overview: Overall, bicycle use increased markedly, while bus use followed a downward trend. Car use remained relatively stable throughout the period.
  • Body paragraphs: The sample detail paragraphs above group the largest changes together, then describe the steadier car figures.

Notice what the plan prevents: a response that begins with isolated numbers and only discovers the overall pattern halfway through. In my experience as a former IELTS examiner, a focused overview and relevant comparisons communicate understanding more clearly than a long list of data.

Use accurate trend language

Line graphs often cover topics such as transport, energy use, population, tourism or internet access. Choose words that match the movement, not words that merely sound impressive.

  • Increase: rise, grow, climb, increase
  • Decrease: fall, decline, drop
  • Little change: remain stable, stay constant
  • Movement with variation: fluctuate
  • Speed or scale: gradually, steadily, sharply, substantially

Use strong verbs only when the graph supports them. A line that changes gently should not soar; a series of small movements may fluctuate rather than rise steadily. Avoid these time-management mistakes

Spending eight minutes on the introduction: It needs only one clear paraphrase. Save your time for the overview and comparisons.

Writing the overview last: Candidates often run out of time or forget it. Draft it immediately after identifying the main features.

Skipping the check: A quick review can catch a tense error, missing unit or unclear comparison.

Recopying a neat draft: Write your answer directly and legibly. Rewriting the whole response wastes valuable minutes.

Practise with a study buddy

Choose one line graph and complete separate timed runs. One person keeps time while both writers follow the six stages. At the end of minute four, compare your notes: did you identify the same main trend, biggest change and stable category?

Then swap answers. The buddy should use the checking list above to review the overview, figures, tense and word count, not rewrite the response. If you are practising alone, follow the same routine with a timer and use the checklist for your own review.

Try the full 20-minute routine

Set a timer and complete one Task 1 line graph response using the plan. Keep notes brief, include a clear overview and leave the final three minutes for checking.

Afterwards, ask yourself: Did you identify the main features before writing? Did you group the figures logically? Is every statement supported by the graph? Did you finish with time to check?
