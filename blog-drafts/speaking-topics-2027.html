Are you taking the IELTS Speaking test in 2027? You might be wondering which topics could come up and what vocabulary you should learn.

As a former IELTS examiner, I'd recommend preparing for a broad range of topics rather than trying to predict your exact questions. Nobody outside the test makers knows the real questions. What you can do is build the language, confidence and flexibility to talk about familiar and unfamiliar subjects.

In this guide, you'll find 15 useful IELTS Speaking topics. Each one has:

  • exam-style Part 1, Part 2 and Part 3 practice questions
  • five useful phrases, with meanings and example sentences
  • one quick activity or tip to help you use the language

The answers to the activities are at the end of the post.

1. Artificial intelligence and technology

Artificial intelligence is becoming part of everyday life, so technology is a useful topic to prepare.

Part 1

  • Do you often use technology in your daily life?
  • Is there a piece of technology you would like to learn to use?
  • Have you ever used an AI tool? What did you use it for?

Part 2

Describe a piece of technology that you find useful.

You should say:

  • what it is
  • when you started using it
  • what you use it for

and explain why you find it useful.

Part 3

  • How has technology changed the way people learn?
  • Are people becoming too dependent on technology?
  • What tasks should always be performed by humans?

Useful vocabulary

  • time-saving: helps you do something faster. Voice messages are really time-saving when I'm walking somewhere.
  • digital literacy: the ability to use technology well and safely. Some older people need support to develop their digital literacy.
  • automation: machines doing work that people used to do. Automation has replaced many repetitive jobs in factories.
  • over-reliance on: depending on something too much. There's a risk of over-reliance on AI for simple decisions.
  • user-friendly: easy to use. The app is so user-friendly that even my grandmother uses it.

  2. Some students show an ______ AI tools, so they stop thinking for themselves.
  3. Online banking is ______ because I don't have to queue.

2. Health and well-being

Prepare to discuss physical health, mental well-being and healthy lifestyles.

Part 1

  • How do you usually relax after work or study?
  • Do you think you have a healthy diet?

Part 2

Describe something you do to stay healthy.

You should say:

  • what you do
  • how often you do it
  • when you started doing it

and explain how it benefits you.

Part 3

  • Why do some people find it difficult to maintain a healthy lifestyle?
  • What can employers do to improve people's well-being?
  • Should governments encourage people to exercise more?

Useful vocabulary

  • a sedentary lifestyle: a life with very little physical activity. Office workers often have a sedentary lifestyle.
  • a balanced diet: eating a healthy mix of different foods. I try to eat a balanced diet, but I have a weakness for chocolate.
  • stay active: keep moving and exercising regularly. Walking to work helps me stay active.
  • burnout: extreme tiredness caused by working too hard for too long. Long hours without breaks can lead to burnout.
  • preventive healthcare: care that stops illness before it starts. Regular check-ups are a simple form of preventive healthcare.

Practise this vocabulary: interactive matching activity

Quick practice: upgrade this answer

"I go to the gym because it is good for me. It is good for my health."

Hint: say how it helps you, use one phrase from the table, and add a small example. Aim for three sentences.

3. Education and lifelong learning

Education is a versatile topic that connects to technology, work and personal development.

Part 1

  • Are you working or studying at the moment?
  • What was your favourite subject at school?

Part 2

Describe a skill you would like to learn.

You should say:

  • what the skill is
  • why you want to learn it
  • how you could learn it

and explain how it might benefit you in the future.

Part 3

  • Is it more difficult to learn new skills as an adult?
  • How effective is online education?
  • Should employers provide more training opportunities?

Useful vocabulary

  • transferable skills: skills you can use in many different jobs. Communication and problem-solving are transferable skills.
  • pick up (a skill): learn something informally, without a course. I picked up some Spanish while travelling.
  • hands-on experience: learning by actually doing something. You can't learn to cook without hands-on experience.
  • at your own pace: as fast or slowly as suits you. Online courses let you learn at your own pace.
  • professional development: training that helps you progress in your job. My company pays for professional development courses.

Practise this vocabulary: interactive matching activity

Quick practice: match the collocations

  1. pick up
  2. gain
  3. learn
  4. develop

a) at your own pace b) a new language c) hands-on experience d) transferable skills

4. The environment and sustainability

You don't need to be an environmental expert, but you should be able to explain everyday environmental problems and possible solutions.

Part 1

  • Do you recycle at home?
  • Do you prefer walking, cycling or using public transport?

Part 2

Describe something you do to help protect the environment.

You should say:

  • what you do
  • when you started doing it
  • how often you do it

and explain why you think it is important.

Part 3

  • What prevents people from living more sustainably?
  • Should companies be responsible for reducing waste?
  • How can environmental awareness be improved?

Useful vocabulary

  • carbon footprint: the amount of CO2 your activities produce. Flying less is one way to reduce your carbon footprint.
  • single-use plastics: plastic items you use once and throw away. Many cafés no longer offer single-use plastics like straws.
  • cut down on: use or do less of something. I'm trying to cut down on the amount of meat I eat.
  • eco-friendly alternatives: options that are less harmful to the environment. Bamboo toothbrushes are an eco-friendly alternative.
  • raise awareness: help people understand a problem. Schools can raise awareness of climate change from an early age.

Practise this vocabulary: interactive matching activity

Quick practice: odd one out

Which word does not fit?

  1. ______ your carbon footprint: reduce / lower / make / cut
  2. ______ awareness: raise / increase / do
  3. ______ single-use plastics: avoid / ban / say / cut down on

5. Work and careers

Modern working arrangements give you plenty to say about advantages, disadvantages and changes in society.

Part 1

  • What do you like most about your job or studies?
  • Would you like to change your job in the future?

Part 2

Describe a job you think would be interesting.

You should say:

  • what the job is
  • what skills it requires
  • what the responsibilities are

and explain why you find it interesting.

Part 3

  • Is job satisfaction more important than salary?
  • Why do some people change careers later in life?
  • How has working from home affected people's lives?

Useful vocabulary

  • work-life balance: a healthy mix of work and free time. Working from home has improved my work-life balance.
  • job security: knowing you're unlikely to lose your job. Government jobs usually offer good job security.
  • career progression: moving up to better positions over time. There isn't much career progression in a small company.
  • flexible working: being able to choose when or where you work. Flexible working is attractive for parents.
  • fulfilling: making you feel satisfied and useful. Teaching can be a very fulfilling job.

Practise this vocabulary: interactive matching activity

Quick practice: which answer is better?

Question: Is job satisfaction more important than salary?

Answer A: "Yes. Job satisfaction is more important. Salary is less important."

Answer B: "I'd say it depends on someone's stage of life. When you're young, enjoying your work and career progression probably matter more. But if you have a family to support, job security and a decent salary become much harder to ignore."

Why is Answer B stronger? Think of two reasons before you check.

6. Travel and tourism

Prepare examples of your own travel experiences and the wider effects of tourism.

Part 1

  • Do you prefer travelling alone or with other people?
  • Where did you go on your last holiday?

Part 2

Describe a place you would like to visit.

You should say:

  • where it is
  • how you heard about it
  • what you would do there

and explain why you would like to visit it.

Part 3

  • How does tourism benefit local communities?
  • What problems can too much tourism cause?
  • Why do people enjoy travelling to unfamiliar places?

Useful vocabulary

  • broaden your horizons: experience new things and ideas. Living abroad really broadened my horizons.
  • off the beaten track: far from the usual tourist places. I prefer small villages off the beaten track.
  • overtourism: too many tourists in one place. Venice is a well-known example of overtourism.
  • cultural heritage: the traditions, buildings and history of a place. The old town is an important part of the city's cultural heritage.
  • boost the local economy: bring more money and jobs to an area. Festivals can boost the local economy.

Practise this vocabulary: interactive matching activity

Quick practice: plan a Part 2 answer in 60 seconds

In the exam you get one minute to make notes. Write keywords, not sentences. For example:

Kyoto · friend's photos · temples, tea ceremony · autumn colours · why: slower pace, history

Now set a timer, make your own notes for this cue card, and then speak for two minutes using only your notes.

7. Social media and communication

Think about how communication has changed and how online interactions differ from face-to-face conversations.

Part 1

  • Which apps do you use to keep in touch with friends?
  • Do you spend a lot of time on social media?

Part 2

Describe a useful website or online platform.

You should say:

  • what it is
  • how you discovered it
  • what you use it for

and explain why you find it useful.

Part 3

  • Has social media improved communication?
  • Why do people share personal information online?
  • How can people recognise misleading information?

Useful vocabulary

  • keep in touch with: stay in contact with someone. I keep in touch with my school friends on WhatsApp.
  • misinformation: false or misleading information. Misinformation spreads very quickly online.
  • privacy concerns: worries about who can see your personal data. Some people avoid Facebook because of privacy concerns.
  • scroll mindlessly: look through content without really paying attention. I sometimes scroll mindlessly before bed.
  • a reliable source: somewhere you can trust for accurate information. I check the news on a reliable source before I share it.

Practise this vocabulary: interactive matching activity

Quick practice: fix the mistake

  1. I keep in contact to my friends through WhatsApp.
  2. Social media has many fake informations.
  3. I always check if the news comes from a trustable source.

8. Money and consumer habits

Prepare to talk about spending, saving, advertising and shopping habits.

Part 1

  • Do you prefer shopping online or in shops?
  • Is it easy for you to save money?

Part 2

Describe something you bought that was worth the money.

You should say:

  • what you bought
  • when you bought it
  • why you decided to buy it

and explain why you think it was good value.

Part 3

  • Why do people sometimes buy things they don't need?
  • Should children be taught how to manage money?
  • How has online shopping changed consumer habits?

Useful vocabulary

  • value for money: worth the price you paid. My laptop was expensive, but it's been excellent value for money.
  • impulse buying: buying something suddenly, without planning. Supermarkets put sweets near the checkout to encourage impulse buying.
  • splash out on: spend a lot of money on something special. I splashed out on a good pair of headphones.
  • on a tight budget: with very little money to spend. As a student, I was always on a tight budget.
  • financial literacy: understanding how to manage money. Financial literacy should be taught at school.

Practise this vocabulary: interactive matching activity

Quick practice: complete the story

Use: splash out on, impulse buy, on a tight budget, value for money

Last year I was ______, so I didn't usually ______ anything. But I decided to buy a good winter coat. It wasn't an ______: I'd been looking for weeks. Three winters later, it still looks new, so it was great ______.

9. Cities and public transport

This topic lets you discuss your neighbourhood, infrastructure and the challenges of city life.

Part 1

  • What do you like about the area where you live?
  • How do you usually get around your city?

Part 2

Describe a place in your town or city where you enjoy spending time.

You should say:

  • where it is
  • what it looks like
  • what people can do there

and explain why you enjoy going there.

Part 3

  • What makes a city a pleasant place to live?
  • How can cities reduce traffic congestion?
  • Should more public spaces be provided in cities?

Useful vocabulary

  • traffic congestion: too many vehicles on the roads. Traffic congestion is terrible during rush hour.
  • green spaces: parks and areas with grass and trees. Every neighbourhood needs green spaces for children to play.
  • pedestrian-friendly: safe and pleasant for people walking. The city centre has become much more pedestrian-friendly.
  • vibrant: full of life and energy. It's a vibrant area with lots of cafés and street markets.
  • the cost of living: how much money you need for basic things. The cost of living in big cities keeps rising.

Practise this vocabulary: interactive matching activity

Quick practice: replace "nice"

"It's a nice park. There are nice trees and the atmosphere is nice."

Rewrite this description without using nice. Hint: think about size, sound, colour and how the place makes you feel.

10. Food and cooking

Food is a familiar topic, but Part 3 questions may ask you to discuss culture, health or changing lifestyles.

Part 1

  • Do you like cooking?
  • What did you eat for breakfast today?

Part 2

Describe a meal you particularly enjoyed.

You should say:

  • what you ate
  • where you ate it
  • who you were with

and explain why you enjoyed it.

Part 3

  • Why are traditional eating habits changing?
  • Should cooking be taught in schools?
  • Why do some people prefer convenience food?

Useful vocabulary

  • home-cooked: made at home, not bought ready-made. Nothing beats a home-cooked meal after a long day.
  • convenience food: food that is quick and easy to prepare. People with long working hours often rely on convenience food.
  • a staple: a basic food that people eat regularly. Rice is a staple in my country.
  • locally sourced: produced near where it is sold. The restaurant only uses locally sourced vegetables.
  • eat on the go: eat while travelling or doing something else. I often eat on the go when I'm busy.

Practise this vocabulary: interactive matching activity

Quick practice: extend your Part 1 answer

A one-word answer is too short, but Part 1 doesn't need a speech. Try this pattern: answer + reason + example.

Question: Do you like cooking?

Short answer: "Yes."

Now write a two or three sentence answer using the pattern and one phrase from the table.

11. Culture and traditions

Prepare examples from your own culture as well as your opinions about cultural change.

Part 1

  • What is your favourite festival or public holiday?
  • Did you celebrate birthdays in a special way as a child?

Part 2

Describe a traditional celebration in your country.

You should say:

  • what the celebration is
  • when it takes place
  • how people celebrate it

and explain why it is important.

Part 3

  • Why is it important to preserve traditions?
  • How does globalisation affect local cultures?
  • Do younger generations celebrate traditions differently?

Useful vocabulary

  • pass down: give from one generation to the next. The recipe has been passed down in my family for years.
  • cultural identity: the feeling of belonging to a culture. Language is a big part of cultural identity.
  • a sense of belonging: feeling that you are part of a group. Festivals give people a sense of belonging.
  • customs: traditional ways of doing things. Some wedding customs are slowly disappearing.
  • the generation gap: differences between younger and older people. The generation gap is clear when it comes to technology.

Practise this vocabulary: interactive matching activity

Quick practice: past and present

Talking about change is a great way to show a range of grammar. Complete the sentence:

When I was a child, we ______ (visit) my grandparents every New Year, but nowadays we ______ (tend) to celebrate at home.

Then make two sentences of your own about a tradition in your country.

12. Friends and relationships

This topic is useful for practising descriptions of people and personal experiences.

Part 1

  • Do you prefer having a few close friends or lots of friends?
  • How often do you see your friends?

Part 2

Describe a person who has helped you.

You should say:

  • who the person is
  • how you know them
  • what they did to help you

and explain how you felt about their help.

Part 3

  • What qualities make someone a good friend?
  • Why do friendships sometimes change over time?
  • Is it easier to make friends today than in the past?

Useful vocabulary

  • get on well with: have a good relationship with. I get on really well with my colleagues.
  • mutual trust: trust that goes both ways. Good friendships are built on mutual trust.
  • lose touch: stop being in contact. We lost touch after university.
  • dependable: someone you can rely on. My sister is the most dependable person I know.
  • go out of your way: make a special effort to help. My neighbour went out of her way to help me move.

Practise this vocabulary: interactive matching activity

Quick practice: match the phrasal verbs

  1. get on with
  2. lose touch
  3. fall out
  4. look up to

a) admire and respect someone b) have an argument and stop being friendly c) have a good relationship d) stop being in contact

13. Entertainment and leisure

Consider how people spend their free time and how their preferences change as they get older.

Part 1

  • What do you usually do at the weekend?
  • Have your hobbies changed since you were a child?

Part 2

Describe a hobby you enjoy.

You should say:

  • what the hobby is
  • how you became interested in it
  • how often you do it

and explain why you enjoy it.

Part 3

  • Why is leisure time important?
  • How has technology changed people's hobbies?
  • Should children have more unstructured free time?

Useful vocabulary

  • unwind: relax after work or stress. I unwind by going for a walk after dinner.
  • a creative outlet: an activity that lets you express yourself. Painting is a great creative outlet for me.
  • get into (something): start to become interested in something. I got into photography during lockdown.
  • screen time: time spent looking at phones, computers or TV. I'm trying to reduce my screen time in the evenings.
  • a sense of achievement: the good feeling of finishing something difficult. Finishing a long hike gives me a real sense of achievement.

Practise this vocabulary: interactive matching activity

Quick practice: speculate in Part 3

Part 3 often asks about other people and the future. You won't always know the answer, and that's fine. These starters help you sound natural:

  • I suspect that…
  • It's probably fair to say that…
  • I'd imagine that…

Use each starter to answer: How has technology changed people's hobbies?

14. Success and personal goals

This topic helps you practise talking about abstract ideas and personal values.

Part 1

  • Do you usually make plans for the future?
  • Is there anything you would like to achieve this year?

Part 2

Describe a goal you have achieved.

You should say:

  • what the goal was
  • why you wanted to achieve it
  • what you did to achieve it

and explain how you felt afterwards.

Part 3

  • How do people define success?
  • What role does failure play in personal development?
  • Does competition encourage people to achieve more?

Useful vocabulary

  • set yourself a goal: decide on something you want to achieve. I set myself a goal of reading one book a month.
  • perseverance: continuing even when something is difficult. Learning a language takes a lot of perseverance.
  • a setback: a problem that delays your progress. Failing my driving test was a setback, but I passed the second time.
  • overcome obstacles: deal successfully with difficulties. Successful people learn to overcome obstacles.
  • self-discipline: the ability to make yourself do things. Working from home requires a lot of self-discipline.

Practise this vocabulary: interactive matching activity

Quick practice: tell the story clearly

Part 2 answers are easier to follow when you signal the order of events. Complete this example with: Eventually, At first, such as

I'd always wanted to run a half marathon. ______, I could barely run five kilometres, so I set myself a training plan. There were a few setbacks, ______ a knee injury in the second month. ______, I crossed the finish line, and I've never felt so proud.

15. Animals and the natural world

Animals and nature can lead to interesting discussions about responsibility, conservation and human behaviour.

Part 1

  • Did you have a pet when you were a child?
  • Do you enjoy spending time in nature?

Part 2

Describe an animal you find interesting.

You should say:

  • what the animal is
  • where it lives
  • what you know about it

and explain why you find it interesting.

Part 3

  • Why do people keep pets?
  • How can endangered animals be protected?
  • Should children spend more time in nature?

Useful vocabulary

  • natural habitat: the place where an animal normally lives. It's sad to see animals kept far from their natural habitat.
  • endangered species: animals at risk of disappearing. The panda used to be listed as an endangered species.
  • animal welfare: the health and happiness of animals. Zoos should put animal welfare first.
  • biodiversity: the variety of plants and animals in an area. Cutting down forests reduces biodiversity.
  • a loyal companion: an animal or person who stays by your side. My dog has been a loyal companion for ten years.

Practise this vocabulary: interactive matching activity

Quick practice: paraphrase the question

A natural way to start a Part 3 answer is to repeat the idea of the question in your own words. It also gives you a few seconds to think.

Question: Why do people keep pets?

Example start: "I think people have animals at home for a few different reasons…"

Now write your own opening for the other two Part 3 questions above. Hint: change one or two key words. Don't repeat the whole question.

What matters more than predicting IELTS Speaking topics?

One mistake students make is spending too much time memorising impressive vocabulary and complete model answers.

As an examiner, I was interested in how effectively candidates communicated their ideas. Complicated words don't automatically lead to a higher band score, and memorised answers are usually easy to spot.

Instead, focus on:

  • answering the question directly
  • developing your ideas naturally
  • using relevant and accurate vocabulary
  • showing a range of grammatical structures
  • speaking clearly and keeping a natural flow

For example, if you are asked whether working from home is beneficial, don't just list three advantages. Explain one advantage properly, give an example and consider whether it applies to everyone.

How to use these topics to prepare

  1. Choose one topic each day.
  2. Read the vocabulary table and say each example sentence out loud.
  3. Do the quick practice activity.
  4. Answer the Part 1 questions, then speak for two minutes on the Part 2 cue card. Record yourself.
  5. Answer the Part 3 questions without preparing them first.
  6. Listen back. Can you hear repeated words, unfinished ideas or grammar mistakes? Did you use at least two phrases from the table?

After all 15 topics, go back to the ones you found most difficult.

Frequently asked questions

Are these the real IELTS Speaking questions for 2027? No. These are independent practice questions. They are not confirmed IELTS questions or predictions. They cover the kinds of everyday and abstract topics that often appear in the Speaking test.

How many topics should I prepare? There's no fixed number. It's more useful to practise a wide range of topics until you can talk comfortably about something unfamiliar than to perfect a few answers.

Should I memorise answers? No. Learn useful phrases and practise using them in different contexts. Memorised answers often sound unnatural and may not answer the exact question you're asked.
